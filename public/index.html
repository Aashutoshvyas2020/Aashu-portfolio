Rift

Founder
2026 to present

  • Website

Rift started from a problem I saw in how attention is modeled.

Most systems analyzing video treat each moment mostly independently, but human attention has memory. What someone notices now is influenced by what they just saw.

I’m building models that use temporal context to better understand how attention changes through short-form video, with the larger goal of turning that understanding into useful tools for creators and content teams.

The research has involved gaze prediction, temporal modeling, cognitive event boundaries, and experiments around what actually changes or resets the memory guiding attention.

Rift

  • Website

Rift models how human attention changes over time while someone watches video.

The central idea is that attention should not be predicted frame by frame as if every moment exists independently. What someone notices now is affected by what they have already seen.

I’m building and testing models with temporal memory to capture that behavior more accurately and studying what kinds of events cause that memory to change.

Emberline

Wildfire situational awareness

Emberline turns satellite thermal observations into persistent, explainable possible-fire events. It filters known industrial heat, checks official incident data, and adds weather and air-quality context for places people care about.

The app distinguishes satellite hotspots from confirmed fires. It is an informational tool, not an evacuation forecast or replacement for official emergency alerts.

Adaptive Search for LLM Creativity

I’m working on a research question around whether an LLM can make better creative decisions by changing how it searches through ideas.

When a model is solving a creative task, there are different useful directions it can take. It can deepen the current idea, explore something much more distant, or consolidate ideas it has already found.

My approach is to represent those as explicit actions and study whether a small controller can learn when each one is most useful.

I’m especially interested in whether useful, non-obvious connections can be searched for systematically instead of being treated as something that only appears by chance.

A major part of the research is also defining how creativity should actually be evaluated. Novelty alone is not enough if the resulting idea is not useful, so the work looks at creativity through quality, diversity, and novelty rather than collapsing everything into one score.

I started photography in 2020.

I originally got into it because I liked finding compositions and places that looked different through a camera than they did normally. Over time I became much more interested in how a photograph controls attention, what gets emphasized, and what gets left outside the frame.

A lot of my work focuses on landscapes, architecture, cities, and traces of people in places.

My photography has received a Gold Key, Silver Key, and two Honorable Mentions through the Scholastic Art & Writing Awards.

I also published my first photography book, What the Eye Returns To.

After Passage

After Passage is a photography project centered around places after people have moved through them.

The photographs focus less on people themselves and more on what remains: structures, roads, objects, landscapes, and small signs of previous human presence.

The project is about what a place can communicate even when the people who shaped it are no longer visible.

What the Eye Returns To

  • Photography
  • Book

What the Eye Returns To is my first photography book.

The book brings together photographs I made across different places and periods rather than documenting one single location.

The title came from something I noticed while editing my work. Some photographs are impressive immediately, but others are the ones I kept returning to.

I became more interested in those images and in understanding why certain scenes continue to hold attention after the first look.
